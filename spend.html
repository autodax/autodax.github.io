The idea behind risk-based guardrails

Instead of watching a withdrawal rate, this method asks a direct question each year: if I keep spending this much (plus inflation), what are the chances my money lasts to my plan-to age? It answers by running thousands of simulated futures with random market returns.

  • If the chance is inside your comfort band (say 72% to 95%), you change nothing.
  • If it drops below the lower guardrail, spending is held at the most you can take while staying at that guardrail.
  • If it rises above the upper guardrail, spending is raised to the amount that puts you at that guardrail. Your target chance (say 85%) is the answer shown when you leave last year's withdrawal blank.

The upside is that it uses your actual age, balance, and Social Security timing, so longevity is built in. The return and volatility boxes fill in automatically from your stock/bond mix, using a saved snapshot of Vanguard's Capital Markets Model run of June 30, 2026: the midpoints of its 10-year return ranges (US stocks 4.2% to 6.2%, so 5.2%; US aggregate bonds 4.3% to 5.3%, so 4.8%) and its median volatility (stocks 15.1%, bonds 6.2%). The 0.1 stock-bond correlation is my own assumption, not a Vanguard figure. It is a snapshot, not a live feed. The downside is that it depends on those assumptions, and a large drop in your balance can call for a large cut. It is an estimate, not a guarantee.

How the historical backtest works

Instead of random simulations, this replays what actually happened. For every possible retirement start year from 1871 on, it applies the real stock and bond returns of the next several decades (up to your plan-to age) to your balance and spending, and counts how many of those retirements would have lasted. That share is your chance of success, and the guardrails work the same way as before.

It is the approach FI Calc uses, on the same Shiller data. Its strengths are real market history, with real crashes and inflation, and no forecast to guess. Its limits: the history is US-only and includes the 20th century's unusually strong results, the periods overlap heavily so there are really only a handful of independent retirements, and it can't condition on today's high valuations. If you choose to wrap around, late start years continue with the oldest data (after 2022 comes 1871), which is artificial because those markets are unconnected, so treat those windows with some caution.

About last year's withdrawal: it is optional. If you enter it, it works as a test. When last year's amount plus inflation still gives a chance of success inside your comfort band, that is your answer. Outside the band, spending is held at the nearest edge of the band, so the answer changes smoothly and in one direction as last year's amount changes. Leave it blank to see the amount that hits your target chance. Use the amount taken from the portfolio only (not Social Security), before tax, and next year enter this year's result. Enter Social Security in today's dollars; it is applied to your spending first so your portfolio covers the rest. This is an estimate, not financial advice.
