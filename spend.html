The idea behind Guyton-Klinger

A fixed rule like "withdraw 4% and add inflation forever" has to be cautious enough to survive the worst markets in history, so in most outcomes it leaves a lot unspent. Guyton-Klinger makes a trade: start higher, and agree to adjust if things go badly.

  • Start at about 5%. The typical starting point is around 5% of your balance in the first year (the original research looked at roughly 5% to 5.5% with a stock-heavy portfolio), compared with the 4% rule.
  • Inflation raises by default. Your spending power is protected in ordinary years, except after a down year when your rate is already above the starting rate.
  • Guardrails keep the rate in a band. If a falling balance pushes your withdrawal rate 20% above the start (about 6%), you cut spending 10%. If a rising balance pulls it 20% below (about 4%), you give yourself a 10% raise.
  • Cuts stop late in life, because with few years left a cut helps little.

The cost is that income is not guaranteed. In a bad stretch you may take a few modest cuts, so this works best if you have some flexibility in spending. The benefit is a higher starting income and a plan that responds to what the markets actually do, rather than one set in stone.

The idea behind risk-based guardrails

Instead of watching a withdrawal rate, this method asks a direct question each year: if I keep spending this much (plus inflation), what are the chances my money lasts to my plan-to age? It answers by running thousands of simulated futures with random market returns.

  • If the chance is inside your comfort band (say 72% to 95%), you change nothing.
  • If it drops below the lower guardrail, spending is held at the most you can take while staying at that guardrail.
  • If it rises above the upper guardrail, spending is raised to the amount that puts you at that guardrail. Your target chance (say 85%) is the answer shown when you leave last year's withdrawal blank.

The upside is that it uses your actual age, balance, and Social Security timing, so longevity is built in. The return and volatility boxes fill in automatically from your stock/bond mix, using a saved snapshot of Vanguard's Capital Markets Model run of June 30, 2026: the midpoints of its 10-year return ranges (US stocks 4.2% to 6.2%, so 5.2%; US aggregate bonds 4.3% to 5.3%, so 4.8%) and its median volatility (stocks 15.1%, bonds 6.2%). The 0.1 stock-bond correlation is my own assumption, not a Vanguard figure. It is a snapshot, not a live feed. The downside is that it depends on those assumptions, and a large drop in your balance can call for a large cut. It is an estimate, not a guarantee.

About last year's withdrawal: Guyton-Klinger adjusts what you took last year, so it is required. With risk-based guardrails it is optional and works only as a test. If last year's amount plus inflation still gives a chance of success inside your comfort band, you keep it. Outside the band, spending is held at the nearest edge of the band, so the answer changes smoothly and in one direction as last year's amount changes. Leave it blank to see the amount that hits your target chance. Use the amount taken from the portfolio only (not Social Security), before tax, and next year enter this year's result. Enter Social Security in today's dollars; it is applied to your spending first so your portfolio covers the rest. This is an estimate, not financial advice.
